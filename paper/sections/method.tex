\section{Method: alternative-dependent diagnosability}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Definitions}\label{sec:defs}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a set of design alternatives. An alternative $a\in\mathcal{A}$ is an architecture together with the control structure it implies---for a generator, the machine topology and the converter control that the topology requires. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a set of failure-mode classes; each class $f\in\mathcal{F}$ is parameterised by an \emph{extent} $e\in E_f$ expressed at the design level, so that the same $(f,e)$ can be realised in every alternative. For a winding fault, the extent is the fraction of a winding that is short-circuited; for a bearing it might be a defect size; for a structure a crack length. Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a set of candidate observation suites; a suite $s\in\mathcal{S}$ is a set of measurements with a cost $c(s)$, and $\Phi(s)$ is the set of features that can be computed from those measurements alone. Let $\mathcal{O}$ be the operating envelope, discretised into operating points $o$.

A \emph{trial} is a record of the system in alternative $a$ at operating point $o$ containing a reference interval $R$ in which the system is healthy and a faulted interval $F$ in which failure mode $(f,e)$ is present. Trials may come from a physical test (test-stage instantiation) or from a validated simulation (design-stage instantiation); the method is indifferent to their origin, and the two instantiations can be combined by using the physical trials to validate the simulated ones.

\subsection{The signal-to-drift ratio}\label{sec:sdr}

For a feature $\varphi\in\Phi(s)$ and a trial, let $\bar\varphi_R$ and $\bar\varphi_F$ be the feature's mean values over the reference and faulted intervals, and define the relative change under fault
\begin{equation}
  \delta_\varphi = \frac{\bar\varphi_F - \bar\varphi_R}{|\bar\varphi_R|}.
  \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
The reference interval is split into two adjacent halves $R_1, R_2$ of the same duration as $F$, and the relative change between them,
\begin{equation}
  \delta^0_\varphi = \frac{\bar\varphi_{R_2} - \bar\varphi_{R_1}}{|\bar\varphi_{R_1}|},
  \label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
measures how much the feature moves when nothing is wrong: sensor noise, drift of the operating point, controller activity, thermal effects. Because every trial has a healthy reference interval, $\delta^0_\varphi$ is available from every trial of alternative $a$, faulted or not, and its absolute value pooled over all $N_a$ trials forms an empirical null distribution of ordinary non-stationarity for that alternative and feature. The signal-to-drift ratio is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SDR}_\varphi = \frac{|\delta_\varphi|}{q_\alpha\!\left(|\delta^0_\varphi|\right)},
  \label{eq:sdr}
\end{equation}
where $q_\alpha$ is the $\alpha$-quantile of the pooled null. With $\alpha=0.95$, $\mathrm{SDR}_\varphi\ge1$ means that the change under fault exceeds what the healthy system produces on its own in 95\,\% of comparable intervals: the fault is detectable at a per-trial false-alarm rate of approximately $1-\alpha$ by the simplest possible detector, a threshold on the relative change of one feature.

Four properties make the SDR suitable as a design measure. It is \emph{scale-free}: a current ratio, a voltage harmonic and a controller output are compared on the same axis, so features from different suites and alternatives whose signals live in different units can be ranked together. It is \emph{calibrated} by construction against each alternative's own non-stationarity, so an alternative with a noisier healthy behaviour is not credited with detectability it does not have. It is \emph{per-trial}, so its distribution over trials carries the dependence on operating point and fault extent that the design quantities below summarise. And it requires \emph{no detector}: it upper-bounds what a threshold detector on that feature can do, and it does so before any algorithm is chosen, which is what makes it usable at the design stage.

Two safeguards are part of the definition. First, features whose SDR exceeds one on healthy trials---trials in which the fault interval contains no fault---more often than a tolerance $\tau$ are flagged as \emph{unreliable} for that alternative and excluded from the design quantities; with nine healthy trials per alternative in the demonstration we use $\tau=0.2$, i.e.\ at most one false alarm. Second, the analyst chooses whether a suite is evaluated by one \emph{fixed} feature (the feature with the highest median SDR over the trials of a failure-mode class, chosen once) or by the \emph{oracle} feature (the best feature of each trial). The fixed variant is what a threshold detector would achieve; the oracle variant is an upper bound for a detector that combines the suite's features. We report both.

\subsection{Derived design quantities}\label{sec:derived}

From the SDR distribution over trials we define, for each alternative $a$, suite $s$ and failure-mode class $f$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{diagnosability map} $D_a(s,f,e,o)$: the SDR of the suite's fixed feature at extent $e$ and operating point $o$;
  \item the \emph{minimum detectable extent} $\mathrm{MDE}_a(s,f) = \min\{e : \operatorname{median}_o D_a(s,f,e,o)\ge 1\}$, the smallest extent whose median SDR over the operating envelope reaches one;
  \item the \emph{detectable share} $\mathrm{DS}_a(s,f)$, the fraction of faulted trials with SDR $\ge1$;
  \item the \emph{signature location}: the group of measurements (e.g.\ stator currents, terminal voltages, controller internals, mechanical quantities) whose best reliable feature attains the highest SDR in the largest share of trials.
\end{itemize}
MDE and DS are the quantities against which requirements can be written (``inter-turn faults of extent $\ge e_{\mathrm{req}}$ shall be detectable over the operating envelope at a per-trial false-alarm rate $\le 1-\alpha$''), and the signature location is the quantity that tells the designer \emph{why} an alternative behaves as it does.

\subsection{Design procedure}\label{sec:procedure}

\input{sections/fig_method}

The procedure (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}) has six steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Frame.} Enumerate the alternatives $\mathcal{A}$, the failure-mode classes $\mathcal{F}$ with a design-level extent scale $E_f$ that is realisable in every alternative, the candidate observation suites $\mathcal{S}$ with their costs, and the operating envelope $\mathcal{O}$.
  \item \textbf{Generate trials.} For each $(a,f,e,o)$ obtain trials with a healthy reference interval and a faulted interval. At the design stage this is a simulation task (a finite-element or circuit model of each alternative with the fault injected at extent $e$); at the test stage it is a prototype or benchmark experiment. Include healthy trials with the same timing so that the reliability screen can be applied.
  \item \textbf{Compute.} Extract the features $\Phi(s)$ for all suites on sliding windows within the intervals, form the pooled null per alternative and feature, compute the SDR per trial, and screen unreliable features.
  \item \textbf{Map.} Build $D_a(s,f,e,o)$ and summarise it into $\mathrm{MDE}_a(s,f)$, $\mathrm{DS}_a(s,f)$ and the signature location, for the fixed and the oracle variants.
  \item \textbf{Select.} For each alternative, find the cheapest suite that meets the detectability requirement; compare the alternatives on cost, MDE and DS together with their other criteria. The output is a co-selection of architecture and observation suite, not a monitoring design for a given architecture.
  \item \textbf{Assess transfer.} If the observation suite and its detector must serve a family of alternatives, test whether a detector built on one alternative's trials detects the others' faults, under the candidate feature-referencing rules (physical units; scaled on the target's healthy trials; referenced to each trial's own healthy interval). The referencing rule that transfers becomes part of the monitoring specification.
\end{enumerate}
Steps 3--4 are mechanical and fully specified by the equations above; steps 1, 2, 5 and 6 are design decisions that the method structures but does not take.

\subsection{Scope}

The method evaluates detectability of a known failure-mode class at a known extent; it does not address fault isolation, severity estimation or remaining-life prediction, each of which presupposes detectability and can be built on the same trials. It also does not model the cost of missed detection; that enters through the requirement in step 5. Finally, the SDR is a property of a feature under a threshold detector: a detector that pools many windows or many features can detect below $\mathrm{SDR}=1$, which is why the oracle variant and the transfer experiment of step 6 are included.
