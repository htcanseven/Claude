\begin{abstract}
Vibration-based condition monitoring on low-cost sensors hinges on two measurement questions that are often conflated: how much fault-relevant information a consumer-grade MEMS accelerometer can actually measure, and how the diagnostic performance of such a sensor should itself be measured. Both are addressed for a smartphone-class accelerometer applied to induction-machine condition monitoring, using a public 100 Hz dataset of six health states, three supply frequencies, and two loads. The sensor is first characterised as a measurement instrument and an information-theoretic channel analysis shows that the on-device gravity compensation discards diagnostically useful low-frequency content, so the raw channels are the more informative. A leakage-free, recording-wise protocol is then introduced to obtain unbiased performance estimates; it reveals that the near-perfect accuracies reported under conventional random-split evaluation are a measurement artefact of window overlap and recording identity, overstating accuracy by 40–45 percentage points. Reported with expanded uncertainty, an accuracy–resource characterisation shows that a resource-frugal configuration (25 Hz, three raw channels, 4 s window) measures machine health at least as reliably as the full-rate configuration while cutting data volume roughly eight-fold, which in turn defines low-bandwidth operating points for IoT nodes over BLE, Zigbee, NB-IoT, and LoRaWAN. The released, uncertainty-aware pipeline provides a reproducible reference for assessing low-cost vibration-sensing instruments.
\end{abstract}
