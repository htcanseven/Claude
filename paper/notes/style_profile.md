# Writing style of the author's papers and how the manuscript follows it

Source: five papers written before the use of AI tools, supplied by the author:

1. Canseven, Pyrhönen J., Pyrhönen J., "Extending Carter factor to slot openings equipped with semimagnetic slot
   wedges", IEEE Trans. Energy Convers. 40(2), 2025 (letter).
2. Canseven, Petrov, Pyrhönen, "Impact of stator core magnetic asymmetry on the properties of a high specific power
   PMSM", IEEE Trans. Ind. Appl. 60(3), 2024.
3. Canseven, Petrov, Martikainen, Pyrhönen, "Eddy-current losses in slitted rotor cores of PMSMs—Development of a novel
   method", IEEE Trans. Energy Convers. 40(2), 2025.
4. Canseven, Ünsal, "Performance improvement of fault-tolerant control for dual three-phase PMSM drives under
   inter-turn short circuit faults", IECON 2021.
5. Bakbak, Canseven, Ayaz, Altıntaş, Meşe, "Maximizing energy extraction from direct grid coupled PMSG for wind energy
   conversion systems", IEEE Trans. Ind. Appl. 58(3), 2022 (co-author).

## The style

**Abstract.** The abstract moves from context to gap to approach. It opens with the field ("In recent years,
high-speed PMSMs have gained popularity due to ..."), names the difficulty ("However, ... presents challenges such
as ..."), states what existing methods cannot do ("Traditional methods for calculating eddy-current losses are
insufficient for such designs"; "... does not take into account ... and should, therefore, be extended"), and then
lists the steps of the study in order ("In this study, firstly, ... is introduced, and it is explained why ... After
that, ... is proposed. Experimental tests are carried out ... After the validation of the proposed method, it is used
in ..."). It gives few numbers.

**Introduction.** The introduction opens broadly, with the technology and its use. It then reasons step by step
through the physical background ("The volume of the active elements ... is proportional to the rated torque ...
Therefore, increasing the rotational speed ... is a desirable way of ..."). The literature is summarized paper by paper
("In [17], a new approach using GA is proposed. Experimental results show that ..."; "Fixed speed and variable speed
operations are compared for induction generators in [17]. According to the study, ..."). A sentence then states the
gap ("However, due to the strong end effects, it is difficult to reliably evaluate ..."; "a comparison study would be
timely between ..."). Next comes the approach ("In this study, firstly, ... Following the comparison, ... In order to
validate the simulation results, ..."; "In this article, first, ... Second, ... Finally, ..."), and then the
organization of the paper ("The paper is organized as follows. Section II outlines ... Section III describes ...
Section IV addresses ...").

**Sections.** A section opens by saying what it does ("This section will discuss these losses."; "In this section,
the calculation method of mean power and derived equations of cut-in speed are presented."; "This section is devoted
to compare ..."). It often closes with a lead into the next ("In light of these results, ... will be analyzed in the
next section"; "Hence, the following section describes how ..."). Equations are introduced in the sentence ("is given
in (1)", "can be written as follows:") and followed by "where ... is ..., ... is ...". Section and subsection titles
are descriptive noun phrases ("Losses in HS-PMSM", "FEM Model and Validation", "Reactive Power Measurements").

**Results.** Results are told through their figures and tables: "Fig. 5(a) shows ..., while Fig. 5(b) illustrates
...", "The results ... are given in Fig. 3(a) and (b), respectively.", "As seen in Fig. 9, ...", "It can be seen in
the figure that ...", "According to the results, ...". Each result is followed by its explanation or consequence:
"This is an expected result because ...", "Therefore, there is a trade-off between ...", "Thus, it can be concluded
that ...", "One of the important conclusions that can be deduced from the table is that ...", "Another important
observation is that ...". The numbers stay in the sentence they support, one or two at a time ("results in a 67%
reduction in the rotor losses at the maximum speed and rated power").

**Conclusion.** The conclusion recapitulates in the past tense, without bullets. It says what was done, then what was
found ("A method for ... was proposed to ... Variation of the reactive power was theoretically analyzed ... It was
found that ... It was noticed that ... In this study, this performance difference was examined; further, it was
emphasized how ..."). It ends with a recommendation for practice ("Based on these findings, if ..., ... should be
selected according to the specific requirements of the application") and with future work ("Future works will focus
on ...").

**Voice and sentences.** The prose is mainly passive with "this study", "this paper" or "this article"; "we" appears
only once, in a derivation and a recommendation of the letter. Sentences are of moderate length and carry one idea
each, and their reasoning is explicit ("because", "due to", "owing to", "since"). The connectives are frequent:
"However,", "Therefore,", "Thus,", "Moreover,", "Furthermore,", "In addition,", "Additionally,", "On the other hand,",
"Consequently,", "As a result,", "Hence,", "Besides,", "In conclusion,", "It should be noted that", "It is important
to note that", "Naturally,". Sequences are marked: "Firstly, ... After that, ... Finally, ...". Abbreviations are
defined at first use, e.g., "finite element analysis (FEA)", and used afterwards. Spelling is American ("modeled",
"analyzed", "behavior", "optimization", "organized", "favor").

**Captions and titles.** Captions describe what is shown and name the panels first: "Comparison of results between ...
for (a) smooth solid core and (b) slitted solid core." Table captions are short noun phrases ("Main dimensions and
parameters of the machine", "Comparison results"). Paper titles are descriptive; one carries a subtitle after a dash
("Eddy-current losses in slitted rotor cores of PMSMs—Development of a novel method"), and none is a question.

## How the manuscript follows it

| Element | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Title | a question first | the descriptive part first, the question as subtitle |
| Abstract | dense statement of the method and results | context, then "However, ...", then "In this study, firstly, ... After that, ...", then the findings |
| Introduction | four dense paragraphs | broad opening, gap, research questions, "In this study, firstly, ... After that, ... Finally, ...", contributions, organization of the paper |
| Related work | lists of citations in long sentences | shorter sentences with connectives, each subsection ending with the gap it leaves |
| Sections | no openings | an opening sentence saying what the section does |
| Equations | inside the sentence | "is given in Eq. (1)", followed by "where ..." |
| Results | numbers chained with semicolons | led by the table or figure ("Table 5 gives ..., As seen in the table, ..."), each result followed by "Therefore, ..." or "This is because ..." |
| Subsection titles | phrases such as "What the floor contains" | descriptive noun phrases |
| Conclusions | three compressed paragraphs | past-tense recapitulation, recommendation for practice, "Future works will focus on ..." |
| Spelling | British | American |
| Captions | panel letters after the description | "(a) ..., (b) ..." first, descriptive |

The content does not change: every number, citation, table, figure and claim of the previous version is kept, with
its hedges, and no result is added.
