# One chapter, one owner

Note for the authors. What changes if every chapter has a single owner, and what we can
offer Wrobel and Saari under that rule.

## The problem, measured

Eight of the thirteen chapters currently have two or more external contributors. Three of
them have three: Chapter 9 (Belahcen, Petrov, Saari), Chapter 10 (Wrobel, Nerg, Belahcen,
and the turbomachinery pair on §10.5) and Chapter 11 (Peltoniemi, Hinkkanen, Aarniovuori).

That arrangement puts the whole integration burden on us. Four people writing disjoint
sections of one chapter produce four notations, four levels of assumed background, four
views of what the reader already knows, and no one but us to reconcile them. It is also
unattractive from the contributor's side: a section is a small, oddly-shaped commitment
inside somebody else's argument, with no control over the parts it depends on.

## The model that already works

Sopanen's three chapters are the exception, and they work because the arrangement is
different in kind. He owns them, he decides who else writes, and he answers for the
result. We deal with one person per chapter; he deals with his own people. The bearing
specialist reaches the book through him, not through us.

Generalised, that is a two-tier model:

- **Owners.** One per chapter, either one of the three authors or one external contributor.
  The owner is our only point of contact for that chapter and is responsible for its
  coherence.
- **Co-authors.** Recruited and coordinated by the owner, not by us. Named at the head of
  the chapter alongside the owner. We never negotiate with them directly.

The cost is honest and should be stated: under this rule every external invitation becomes
a chapter of 28 to 40 pages rather than a section of 6 to 12. Some people who would accept
a section will decline a chapter. Against that, an owner who recruits two co-authors may
write less than they would have written as a lone section contributor, and they get a
chapter credit rather than a mention.

## What to offer Rafal Wrobel

**Chapter 10 in full: Thermal management and cooling of high-speed machines. 28 pages,
18 figures, ten sections.**

His reply listed winding heat paths and thermal resistances, advanced stator cooling
concepts, direct conductor cooling, and the thermal limitations of rotor structures and
interfaces. That is §10.1 to §10.4 and §10.9 already. The rest of the chapter, thermal
networks and their calibration, transient behaviour under drive cycles, magnet temperature
and demagnetisation margin, and air-gap convection, is all within a machine-thermal
specialist's range, and where it is not he can bring someone.

What we would tell him we expect from the chapter:

1. That cooling architecture is treated as a design variable, not as a remedy applied
   afterwards. The architecture fixes the admissible loss density, which fixes the electric
   loading, which fixes the size of the machine.
2. That the rotor gets equal weight with the stator. The retaining sleeve that Chapter 4
   requires for mechanical integrity is a thermal insulator, and the magnet temperature
   behind it is usually the binding constraint.
3. That the architectures are presented as choices with the loss density each can sustain
   and what each costs to manufacture, rather than as a survey of case studies.
4. That it closes on the two running machines, as every design chapter does.

This is a clean offer and it should be made. It also resolves two loose ends: Nerg need not
be approached at all unless Wrobel wants him, and the air-gap convection question becomes
Wrobel's to settle with the turbomachinery people rather than ours.

## What to offer Juha Saari

Harder, because he has just asked for less rather than more, and no chapter is smaller than
28 pages. Three possibilities.

**A. Chapter 2 in full: Applications and system architectures. 34 pages.** He spans the
industrial side completely, compressors, blowers, ORC, and now high-temperature heat pumps
from Upheat. He does not span traction, aerospace or e-turbochargers, so he would need
co-authors for those, which is exactly what he offered when he wrote that he could suggest
other names. The argument to put to him is that one chapter he controls is less work than
three sections scattered through three chapters, each with its own context, deadline and
cross-references, even though the page count is similar.

**B. Co-author within someone else's chapter.** He writes the windage and air-gap material
inside Belahcen's Chapter 9, or inside Wrobel's Chapter 10, invited by that owner rather
than by us. This matches what he asked for and costs him least. It also means we do not
negotiate with him at all, which is the point of the model.

**C. Both, in sequence.** Offer him Chapter 2, and if he declines the scale, fall back to B.

Recommended: **A, with B named in the same email as the fallback.** He is the only
industrial contributor who can carry an applications chapter, and losing the applications
chapter to the authors would weaken the part of the book the editor asked about most. But
the email should say plainly that a smaller role is equally welcome, so that a no to the
chapter is not a no to the book.

## The Hinkkanen problem

Chapter 11 is 40 pages split three ways: Peltoniemi on the converter, Hinkkanen on control,
Aarniovuori on harmonic losses. Under the rule it must have one owner, and Peltoniemi has
already agreed to lead it.

Three ways out.

1. **Hold Hinkkanen's invitation.** Peltoniemi's email already asks him to build the team.
   If he names a control person, the question is closed. If he would welcome Hinkkanen,
   Hinkkanen comes in as his co-author, which a professor at another university may or may
   not accept.
2. **Split Chapter 11** into a converter chapter (Peltoniemi, about 24 pages) and a control
   chapter (Hinkkanen, about 18 pages). Clean ownership, and it makes fourteen chapters at
   the same page total. The objection is real: the book argues that machine and drive must
   be treated together, and putting the converter and its control in separate chapters
   reintroduces a boundary of the kind the book criticises.
3. **Do not invite Hinkkanen.** Peltoniemi owns the whole chapter and finds his own control
   contributor at LUT.

Recommended: **1**. His invitation has not gone out. Ask Peltoniemi first who he wants;
his answer decides it, and it costs nothing to wait.

## Consequences elsewhere

- **Chapter 9** becomes Belahcen's alone. If Saari takes Chapter 2, Belahcen invites
  whoever writes the windage material, and Petrov's rotor-loss sections are agreed between
  them rather than assigned by us.
- **The turbomachinery pair** are no longer our invitees. They become co-authors whom Saari
  brings to Chapter 2 or Wrobel brings to Chapter 10.
- **Janne Nerg** is not approached. If Wrobel wants a thermal-networks collaborator, that is
  his choice to make.
- **Ikäheimo and Aarniovuori** are unaffected. They contribute to chapters the authors own,
  which is a different case: we are the owner, so there is no third party to reconcile.
- **The contributor table** should show owners and co-authors as two ranks rather than one
  flat list. A reviewer reading a table of eleven names against thirteen chapters sees a
  committee; the same names in two ranks show a structure.

## An open item that outranks all of this: the Voltcar data

The Voltcar traction machine is the book's mobile running machine. It is specified in
Chapter 2, carried through Chapters 4 to 11, and assembled into a complete measured design
in §13.1. Nine chapters depend on it, and it is half of the two-paradigm structure that
organises the whole text.

Nobody has yet asked the project whether its results may be published in a book. Jenni
Pippuri-Mäkeläinen of VTT manages Voltcar, so she is the person who grants that or brokers
it with the consortium. This has to be settled whether or not she writes anything, and it
should be settled before the proposal goes to Wiley, because a late no would not cost a
section: it would cost the mobile half of the book.

It is the same class of risk as the compressor measurements, and larger, because the
compressor case is still unattached while Voltcar is already load-bearing. Ask early, and
ask what the consortium agreement says rather than only what she personally thinks.

## The other running machine is still unattached

The industrial running machine is described throughout as "a two-pole gearless compressor
drive" and has no confirmed source. Saari was the assumed one; he is taking a chapter
rather than the case study, and has in any event moved employer. Ikäheimo at ABB is a
candidate, and a machine documented in a published dissertation is another.

A published machine has one decisive advantage over an industrial one: **it is already
cleared.** No employer has to be asked, no consortium has to agree, and the figures can be
reproduced with a citation. Set against the compressor case, whose clearance is the largest
unresolved risk in the plan, that is worth a great deal.

What the industrial running machine has to carry, chapter by chapter, before it can be
adopted:

| Chapter | What it must supply |
|---|---|
| 2 | A genuine industrial application, judged on availability and total cost of ownership, not a laboratory demonstrator |
| 4 | Rotor retention: sleeve or bridge design, interference fits, overspeed margin |
| 5 | Rotordynamics: bearing span, critical speeds, mode shapes, sub- or supercritical operation |
| 6 | Bearing type, lubrication arrangement, and ideally life or service experience |
| 7 | Complete electromagnetic dimensions, enough to re-run the sizing procedure |
| 8 | Conductor and winding design |
| 9 | Measured losses, segregated |
| 10 | Cooling arrangement and measured temperatures |
| 11 | The converter it runs on: device family, switching frequency, resulting pulse ratio |
| 12 | Assembled design, measured performance, and ideally qualification against standards |

Two further tests. It should be **recent enough** that a reviewer does not read the book as
retrospective, since the editor asked for updated applications. And it should sit clearly
in the industrial paradigm, so that the contrast with the mobile machine carries the weight
the book puts on it.

A doctoral dissertation is an unusually good source for this, because it normally documents
mechanical, electromagnetic, thermal and drive aspects of one machine in a single place,
which is exactly what a running machine needs and what a journal paper never provides.

## Assessed: the 2 MW solid-rotor machine of Chong Di's dissertation

*Modeling and Analysis of a High-Speed Solid-Rotor Induction Machine*, Chong Di, Acta
Universitatis Lappeenrantaensis 902, LUT, 2020. Supervised by Juha Pyrhönen and Ilya
Petrov. 153 pages, article-based.

| | |
|---|---|
| Rated power | 2 000 kW |
| Synchronous speed | 12 000 r/min, 200 Hz, one pole pair |
| Rated voltage, current | 660 V, 2 100 A rms, delta, two parallel branches |
| Rated torque | 1 600 Nm; power factor 0.7; estimated efficiency 96 % |
| Rotor | solid S355, outer diameter 266 mm, 44 slits 4 mm wide and 50 mm deep |
| Stator | inner 280 mm, outer 700 mm, active length 538 mm, 36 slots, M270-35 |
| Support | active magnetic bearings |
| Cooling | direct liquid cooling, prefabricated coils |
| Tip speed | **167 m/s** |
| fP | **400 000 kW/s, 2.2 times the threshold of §1.1.1** |
| l/D | 1.92 |

**It fits the industrial paradigm almost exactly.** Two poles, as the plan already assumes.
A solid-rotor induction machine, which Chapter 3 calls the industrial workhorse and which
Ikäheimo is writing §3.2 and §3.3 about, so the running machine and the topology chapter
would reinforce each other. Two megawatts is industrial scale, not a demonstrator. At 200 Hz
it sits precisely in the region Figure 1.9 marks as the low-pole industrial topology
reachable with silicon converters, so it demonstrates the industrial branch of the
gatekeeper argument rather than contradicting it. And 167 m/s is above the hundred metres a
second at which the air gap stops being passive, so the windage material has something to
bite on.

**It feeds the chapters unusually well.** Active magnetic bearings, including
unbalanced-magnetic-pull compensation, for §6.5. Direct liquid cooling for Chapter 10, which
is Wrobel's own specialism. Semimagnetic wedges as a slot-geometry decision for Chapter 7.
An asymmetric winding with prefabricated coils and current-unbalance suppression for
Chapter 8. Solid-rotor eddy-current harmonic loss modelling, the dissertation's central
contribution, for §9.5 to §9.7. It also contrasts with the Voltcar machine in the right way:
solid steel and slits against magnets and a sleeve, which makes the industrial-versus-mobile
comparison in every design chapter a real comparison rather than a rhetorical one.

**Clearance is not an issue.** It is published, it is LUT, and two of the three authors
supervised it. Nobody has to be asked.

**The one problem: no measured machine performance.** The measurements in the dissertation
are material-level, core-loss curves fitted to Bertotti at 100, 200 and 400 Hz. The
validation of the machine itself is by finite-element analysis. The text discusses rotor
construction options for "the first prototype", which reads as a design that had not yet
been built when the dissertation was written.

That matters because §12.1 promises the industrial machine carried *from specification to
measured performance*, and Chapter 12 is one of the two places where the book claims
measured case data.

**The question for Juha and Ilya, which only they can answer:** was this machine built, and
are test results available, whether in later work, at a partner, or unpublished? If yes,
adopt it. If no, there are two options, and the first is better:

1. Use it as the industrial running machine through Chapters 2 to 11, where its design is
   fully documented, and pair it in Chapter 12 with whatever measured industrial machine can
   be obtained, from ABB through Ikäheimo. The chapter then has a designed machine and a
   measured one, which is honest and still useful.
2. Adopt it throughout and change §12.1 to read "from specification to validated design",
   keeping "to measured performance" for Voltcar alone. This costs the symmetry of the
   two-machine claim and a reviewer may notice.

Even with the caveat, this is a considerably better candidate than the unattached compressor
drive currently in the plan. Recommend adopting it subject to that one question.
