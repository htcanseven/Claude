# Chapter 10 — candidate contributors beyond LUT

Note for discussion among the authors. **Affiliations below are from published work and may
be out of date; verify each before approaching anyone.** People move, retire and change
roles, and nothing here has been checked against a current institutional page.

## What the chapter actually needs

Chapter 10, *Thermal management and cooling architectures*, 28 pages. The sections that
would go to an outside specialist are the heat paths and thermal resistances (10.2), the
stator cooling architectures from water jackets through in-slot to direct winding cooling
(10.3), rotor cooling and the retaining sleeve as a thermal barrier (10.4), lumped-parameter
thermal networks with their contact resistances and calibration (10.6), and transient
behaviour under overload and drive cycles (10.7). About 13 to 21 pages depending on how
much the authors keep.

The specialism is narrow: lumped-parameter thermal modelling of *electrical machines*,
with the cooling hardware that goes with it. General heat-transfer expertise is not the
same thing and does not substitute.

## Candidates

### Andrea Cavagnino, and Aldo Boglietti — Politecnico di Torino

The academic authority on electrical machine thermal analysis. The Boglietti–Cavagnino–
Staton review of thermal analysis methods for electrical machines is the paper the whole
field cites as its starting point, and their group has produced the systematic work on
thermal parameter identification and on the accuracy of lumped-parameter networks. If one
name is wanted for §10.6, this is where to look first. Verify current status before
approaching; Boglietti is long-established and may no longer be taking on commitments.

Fit: 10.2, 10.6, 10.7. Strong. Adds Italy to an otherwise Nordic list.

### Rafal Wrobel — Newcastle University (previously Bristol)

The best fit for the *modern* cooling content rather than the modelling. His work is on
thermal management at high loss density: direct winding cooling, in-slot cooling, heat
paths through the winding, and the use of additive manufacturing to build them. That is
§10.3 and §10.4 almost exactly, and it is the part of the chapter that dates fastest and
therefore most needs someone currently working on it.

Fit: 10.3, 10.4. Strong, and complementary to the Torino group rather than overlapping.

### David Gerada — University of Nottingham

Co-author of the review of high-speed electrical machine technologies that is the standard
reference for this whole field, and a researcher whose work spans the high-speed thermal
problem specifically rather than machine thermal analysis in general. On subject-matter fit
to *this book* he is arguably the strongest name on the list.

He is also the person most likely to write a competing title, which cuts both ways. Bringing
him in would remove the most credible competitive threat and put the field's best-known
high-speed reviewer inside your book; approaching him also tells him what you are planning.
That is a judgement for the three of you, not a technical question.

Fit: 10.1 and 10.4, and he would strengthen Chapters 2 and 3 as well.

### Mircea Popescu, and David Staton — Ansys (formerly Motor Design Ltd)

Staton is the third author of the review named above and the person behind the
lumped-parameter thermal tool that most of the industry actually uses; Popescu has led much
of the published thermal work from the same group. Unmatched practical depth on §10.6.

The caution is commercial. A thermal chapter written by the vendor of the dominant thermal
tool risks reading as documentation for that tool, and Wiley reviewers notice. Workable if
the chapter is written method-first with no product named, but it needs saying up front.

Fit: 10.6, 10.7. Strong technically; manage the neutrality question.

### Ayman EL-Refaie — Marquette University (formerly GE Global Research)

Works on high specific power machines for aerospace, where thermal management is the binding
constraint rather than a subsystem. Two reasons to consider him beyond the chapter itself:
he could serve the aerospace case study in §13.2, which is currently an open slot, and an
IEEE Fellow with a GE background carries weight with a publisher deciding whether a book has
industrial reach.

Fit: 10.3 and 10.4 from the mass-limited side; 13.2. Fills two gaps with one invitation.

### Yew Chuan Chong

Has worked specifically on machine cooling and ventilation, including the flow and heat
transfer side that connects to §10.5. A narrower, less prominent name than the others, and
correspondingly more likely to say yes.

Fit: 10.3, 10.5 alongside the turbomachinery contributors.

## Recommendation

**Decouple the two asks.** A letter of support costs a senior person twenty minutes; writing
a chapter costs months. The hit rate is entirely different, and the proposal benefits from
both. Ask a wider circle for letters and a narrower one for chapters.

For the chapter itself, the ranking on fit is **Wrobel** for the cooling architectures,
**Cavagnino** for the thermal networks, and **Gerada** if the strategic question resolves in
favour of approaching him. Any one of the three transforms how the contributor list reads:
at present it is LUT plus one Aalto name plus three Finnish industrial contributors, which a
reviewer may read as a single-department book. One well-known non-Nordic contributor changes
that more than three more Finnish ones would.

**Go through Juha.** He knows this community personally, and an approach from him lands
differently from a cold email. The realistic sequence is a word from Juha, then the formal
invitation and the draft letter of support.

**Keep Nerg in view.** A known colleague who will deliver twenty-one pages on schedule is
worth more than a distinguished name who agrees and then does not write. The two are not
exclusive: an outside specialist on the cooling architectures, with Nerg on the thermal
networks and one of the authors owning the chapter, is a stronger arrangement than either
alone and asks less of each person.
